What work went into the atlas

  1. Reconstructed and checked the route. Used the original RFP KMZs for all 52 source features. Checked the atlas vertices against the source and measured along each feature in a local projected coordinate system, UTM 16N in Illinois and 17N in Ohio. Stations are measured from the start of each individual feature. The 319,773 ft atlas length sums the rounded feature lengths.
  2. Screened road control, parcel exposure and right-of-way. Compared route stations to county and municipal bounds, DOT road inventories and available county parcel and recorded ROW data. Road maintenance, legal property rights and permit authority were treated as separate questions. Some station assignments are inferred from GIS. The original atlas builder’s full sample ledger and workbook are not in this package.
  3. Recomputed rail and water intersections across every route feature. Intersected the original linework against FRA/NARN rail geometry and the Ohio and Illinois official flowline layers. Reproduced 11 rail intersection points and found 36 water-line points, including crossings on bore-labeled features that the earlier atlas omitted. A repeated flowline can cross the alignment at several stations. NARN reporting marks provide railroad leads and property records establish the signatory.
  4. Checked the physical setting and controlling records. Used 85 indexed USGS/USDA NAIP image crops to review bridge, drainage, rail, access and staging conditions. Cross-referenced structure IDs and nearby parcel IDs, and screened NWI within 100 m and FEMA at the specific water point. The FEMA service returned results for 24 of 36 points. Twelve require an effective panel check. Image acquisition dates vary and were not independently resolved.
  5. Audited each of the 36 water pins at its exact coordinates. Reviewed a 200 m NAIP crop centered on each original intersection and an independent Ohio statewide aerial for all 33 Ohio pins. Compared the alignment to visible banks, embankments and outlets, then checked Illinois structure records, Ohio bridge and conduit inventories, and county plans. A bridge span or visible surface channel supports an open crossing. A documented culvert or sewer supports buried drainage, even when the channel is open on either side. Pins without a matched physical feature stay in the register as unverified GIS hits. Structure offsets are approximate and need survey before design.
  6. Followed up the ten GIS-only pins with drainage records. Reviewed municipal project drawings, stormwater exhibits, county recorded plats and easements, the ODOT Plan Vault index and Delaware Soil & Water storm and ditch inventories. Overlaid record geometry on the bore alignment and Ohio aerials. The register distinguishes record-backed drainage, unresolved sites and pins removed from the stand-alone bid count. Public exhibits are identified by type. Design sheets, recorded easements and asset inventories do not establish final installed pipe dimensions or clearance.
  7. Translated the screening into construction and permit choices. Compared independent HDD, culvert-clearance bores, existing duct, bridge attachment and local realignments. Researched agency permit intake, applicable rules, agreement and project records. Each of the 36 crossing cards identifies the blocker, likely approval path, required exhibits and fallback. Agency confirmation, survey and final work limits govern which reviews apply.
  8. Reconciled mapped points and unresolved leads. Kept 17 separate unresolved water sites and structure records in the engineering register. Some may be the same asset or work location as a mapped hit. They remain inputs for plan retrieval and field checks.

What still controls the design

Elk Grove Village was the 2008 stormwater permit applicant and can help locate contract and closeout records. Confirm the box owner, survey its outside envelope and the adjacent utilities, and check the full work area against the ditch, outfall, wetland and flood limits.
